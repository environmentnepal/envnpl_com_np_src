A shy bird found only in Nepal is waiting for the world to notice

Read full article at Mongabay Nepal →

Nepal’s shrubby hillsides are home to a medium-sized bird with brownish upperparts, distinctive black streaks from the shafts of its feathers and, sometimes, a chalky white facial patch.

It’s the spiny babbler (Turdoides nipalensis), a mysterious bird, recorded only in Nepal. In June, the bird made headlines with asighting for the first timein the forests of Madhesh province, in Nepal’s southern lowlands. With this recording, the bird has now been spotted in all of Nepal’s seven provinces.

In Kathmandu, sightings of the bird have been recorded from the fringes of Shivapuri Nagarjun National Park to the foothills of Phulchowki, as well as at various locations in and around Pokhara, usually at an elevation ranging from915 to 2,135 meters(3,000 to 7,000 feet) above sea level depending on the season. But in June, ornithologists Hathan Chaudhary and Suraj Baral identified the bird in Madhesh for the first time, at an elevation of around 443 m (1,450 ft). This sighting means the spiny babbler has now been documented across all of Nepal’s seven provinces.

Despite its limited known habitat, the spiny babbler remains poorly understood and researchers and ornithologists are calling for greater global attention to study and protect it.

“We still don’t know a lot about the bird and have no scientific data to back its population, movements, exact breeding season/months and the effects of increasing human settlements,” said Kamal Raj Gosai, who heads the department of environmental science at Kathmandu’s Tri-Chandra Multiple Campus. Gosai is researching the distribution of spiny babbler and the impacts of land use and climate change on the species. “There’s more focus on vulnerable and endangered species and megafauna, and little concern and limited research on this endemic species, both nationally and globally,” he added.

British naturalist Brian Houghton Hodgson first scientifically described the spiny babbler,kade bhyakurin Nepali, in 1836 while living in Kathmandu. His encounter with the one-of-a-kind bird remained a one-off for more than a century, until the 1940s, when Desiree Proud, the wife of a British diplomat, spotted them in her Kathmandu garden and American ornithologist Sidney Dillon Ripley sighted the species in far-western Nepal, confirming its existence.
