Nepal’s newest national parks drive tiger recovery, but new concerns arise

Read full article at Mongabay Nepal →

KATHMANDU — Nepal is now home to an estimated 429 wild tigers, up from 355 four years ago and nearly four-fold from 16 years ago, the country’s conservation officials reported on the occasion of World Tiger Day.

As the country celebrates this historic achievement, a closer look at the latest numbers reveals an uneven recovery. Nepal’s youngest national parks are driving nearly all the growth, while Bardiya, the country’s second-oldest park, has fewer tigers. This could lead to more conflict between not just people and tigers, but people and leopards as well in and around the new national parks, according to recent studies.

Three factors appear to be behind this split: water availability, animal movement both within and outside the country, and spread of invasive species.

“The results from the survey reflect the positive outcomes of ongoing efforts in the field of tiger conservation in Nepal, such as habitat conservation and management, poaching control, conservation of prey species, and biological corridor conservation,” said Bed Kumar Dhakal, spokesperson for the Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation.

Chitwan, the country’s oldest national park, still leads by far in raw numbers. But the newer parks (Banke, Parsa and Shuklaphanta) are where growth is concentrated, while the country’s two oldest parks, Chitwan and Bardiya, are showing the slowest growth or an outright decline.

The numbers are rooted in a global pledge made in November 2010. With wild tiger numbers at an all-time low at roughly 3,200, leaders from the world’s 13 tiger range countries gathered in St. Petersburg, Russia, for the first international tiger summit, where they signed the St. Petersburg Declaration and committed to double the global wild tiger population by 2022. Nepal, then home to an estimated 121 tigers, accepted the pledge and met it by reporting 355 tigers in 2022.

A cursory reading of the latest survey data suggests that while older parks are reaching stagnation, newer ones are emerging as areas of potential growth, said researcher Saneer Lamichhane, a PhD candidate at the wildlife ecology and conservation department, University of Florida, U.S.
