KATHMANDU — In the village of Pithauli, on the edge of Nepal’s Chitwan National Park, a safe feeding site for critically endangered vultureshas long been hailed as a success storyby conservationists.

For visitors, the feeding site, also known as a “vulture restaurant”, provides a rare photo op for tourists: As white-rumped vultures (Gyps bengalensis) and slender-billed vultures (Gyps tenuirostris) feed on carcasses laid out by locals, greater one-horned rhinos, jackals, wild boars and crows watch and, sometimes, participate.

For ornithologist Rajendra Suwal, however, this ensemble is an eyesore, he told Mongabay, “If an infected bird comes to the site, the disease could spread to other birds or even animals.”.

Suwal’s concern stems from a recent outbreak of bird flu or avian influenza in the country’s capital.An outbreak that started in mid-June at Kathmandu’s Central Zoois believed to have cost the lives of at least 65 animals and birds across more than 20 species. The dead included barn owls, Himalayan griffons, greylag geese, jungle cats, leopard cats, a sarus crane and two common leopards.

An investigation by the National Trust for Nature Conservation (NTNC), which manages the zoo, says the origin of the outbreak could likely be traced to two sources: Among birds, it likely started through feral crows roosting in open-topped aviary enclosures, while for carnivores, it spread through chicken meat fed to them by the zoo.

Notably, the zoo’s six tigers (Panthera tigris) were unaffected. A member of NTNC’s investigation committee told Mongabay that this is not necessarily because one species is more resistant. “Tigers at the zoo are fed roughly 5-7 kilograms of buffalo meat each per day, not chicken, so they were never on the same exposure pathway as the infected carnivores,” said the committee member, who asked not to be named as they weren’t authorized to talk to the media.

In national parks, however, carnivores often share common diets. There are also no enclosures to inspect, no feeding records to audit and no vet team to notice the first death, ornithologist Suwal said. Central Zoo lost many animals before it could contain the outbreak, but a park may not know it has an outbreak at all, he added.
